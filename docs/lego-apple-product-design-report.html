Executive Summary

LEGO proved that a single, honest connector — the stud — could turn a pile of parts into anything a person could imagine, and that the joy was in the rebuilding. Apple proved that everyday hardware could be quiet, premium, and obvious to use without a manual. Almost nobody has combined the two for problems adults actually have: a desk that never fits its cables, safety hardware that looks clinical the day you need it most, a tracker a kid loses interest in, a garden that dies from good intentions. This dossier proposes ten products built on that gap.

Every concept shares one design law: the connector carries function, not just structure. Where LEGO studs only hold parts together, ours also carry power, data, water, or sound between bricks — so a "modular" product here is genuinely reconfigurable, not just molded to look like it is.

Day 2 — Market Gaps

Directional planning signals synthesized from public category reports and past consumer-behavior studies on renting, repair, and aging-in-place — treated as hypotheses for the usability testing in Phase 3 to confirm, not as primary survey data collected by this sprint.

  • Renters don't install. A majority of renters avoid drilled, permanent fixtures — the exact hardware category (grab bars, shelving, lighting) that most needs to feel non-committal.
  • Repair fatigue is real. Right-to-repair sentiment is rising faster than the hardware industry's response; single-board electronics that die whole are increasingly a dealbreaker, not a shrug.
  • Safety hardware still looks clinical. Aging-in-place demand is growing quickly, but the visible product language (steel grab bars, med-alert pendants) is exactly what makes people delay buying it.
  • Kids lose things; they don't lose interest in building things. Tracker adoption for kids' gear lags adult adoption — the object itself has no pull for a child.
  • Nobody owns "adult modularity." LEGO owns modularity as play. No brand owns it as a hardware design language for daily problems.
Day 3 — Problem Statement

Consumers increasingly rent rather than own, discard rather than repair, and settle for fixed-shape hardware that never quite matches how their needs actually change. The tactile, rebuild-it-yourself satisfaction that made LEGO a design icon has never been seriously applied to daily hardware. The opportunity: a modular, repairable, Apple-finished hardware language for the problems grown-ups actually have.

Top 10 Product Concepts

Thirty concepts were sketched across the seven category gaps above; these ten were shortlisted on feasibility, impact, and originality (see scoring, §04). Each spread carries a full spec, a dimensioned technical drawing, a scene brief for photography, and a build plan to a working prototype.
03

Usability Testing Protocol

Before any concept moves past its first working prototype, each runs the same five-user protocol — recruiting for the product's actual target user (e.g. parents + one child for TrackTag Bricks; an occupational therapist referral for CareRail). This is the test plan the development team runs in Phase 3, not a substitute for it: no shortcuts on real sessions with real hardware in hand.

StepWhat happens
Recruit5 participants matching the product's stated target user; screen out anyone on the design team or who saw earlier concept sketches.
Baseline2-minute unprompted handling of the physical prototype — what do they try first, with no instructions given?
TaskOne realistic task (e.g. "reconfigure this from a stereo pair into a single speaker") timed and observed without help for the first attempt.
ProbeProduct-specific risk question — see each spec's "test this first" line below.
DebriefWould they buy it, at the stated price, to replace what they use today? Why or why not, in their own words.
SynthesizeFindings folded back into the spec before CAD is locked — this protocol runs again after any material design change.

Build first

CareRail and Pusterom Breath Brick tie for the top score — one because it opens a large, underserved, high-margin category nobody has designed well; the other because it is the cheapest and fastest to a convincing working prototype, and shares a brand story with an existing product in this portfolio. TrackTag Bricks rounds out the first wave: highest mainstream demand signal, moderate build complexity. Recommend committing Weeks 1–10 of real studio time to these three before greenlighting the remaining seven.

Sustainability commitments
  • Every concept specifies recycled, bio-based, or compostable primary material — no virgin single-use plastic in any BOM.
  • Field-repairable cartridges (LumaBrick, BrickCharge Hub) are standard practice going forward, not a one-off feature.
  • Electronics cores are designed to outlive their shells across every tracker- and sensor-based concept, cutting e-waste at the part, not the product.
Next steps
  1. Green-light CAD lock and long-lead tooling quotes for the first-wave three.
  2. Recruit and run the five-user protocol on first working prototypes by end of Week 6.
  3. Fold findings back into spec before committing to injection-mould tooling.
  4. Revisit the remaining seven concepts against first-wave learnings before their own Day-7 kickoff.
